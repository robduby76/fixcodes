P0194: Fuel Rail Pressure Sensor Circuit Intermittent (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-25 · Based on 2 cited source(s)

⚡ Quick answer

P0194 means the engine computer is seeing an intermittent or erratic signal from the fuel rail pressure sensor. OBD-Codes.com lists real high or low fuel pressure, a damaged sensor, circuit resistance, vacuum leaks and low fuel as causes, while RepairPal lists the PCM, the sensor and wiring and names the fuel pump as the common repair. Check fuel level, connectors and vacuum hoses first, then compare the sensor reading against a real gauge.

🚨 Safety first

A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire: slow down and stop driving soon. A red brake or oil pressure warning needs immediate attention. Pull over safely and don't keep driving.

What P0194 means

The fuel rail pressure (FRP) sensor tells the engine computer (PCM) how much pressure is going to the fuel injectors, so it can meter the right amount of fuel for power and fuel economy. RepairPal says P0194 sets when the PCM detects an erratic signal from the sensor. OBD-Codes.com describes it as the incoming sensor signal not matching what is actually being delivered to the engine, and says it is a generic code for most fuel-injected engines, gas and diesel, since about 2000. It can be a mechanical or an electrical fault depending on the vehicle and fuel system. The two sources lean differently: OBD-Codes.com puts actual high or low fuel pressure, vacuum leaks and running low on fuel at the top of its list along with the sensor and circuit resistance, while RepairPal lists only the PCM, the sensor and wiring as causes but still names a fuel pump replacement as the common repair. Because the fault is intermittent, loose or corroded connections are worth checking carefully.

Severity: can I keep driving?

Stop and fix promptly

RepairPal puts P0194 in its urgent category, meaning action is needed right away because it can cause severe damage or put the people in the car at risk. OBD-Codes.com lists lack of power and an engine that cranks but won't start, and RepairPal lists decreased engine performance. Losing power in traffic is dangerous, so get it diagnosed promptly, and have it towed if the engine won't start or loses power badly. Diesel common-rail systems run extremely high fuel pressure, so do not open or loosen anything on the fuel rail yourself.

Common symptoms

  • Check engine light on (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Lack of power or decreased engine performance (both sources)
  • Engine cranks but won't start (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Increased fuel consumption (RepairPal)

Likely causes

Roughly most to least common, based on the sources below. Your vehicle may differ.

  1. Actual fuel pressure too high or too low (OBD-Codes.com)
  2. Faulty fuel rail pressure sensor (both sources)
  3. Excessive resistance in the circuit, or damaged wiring or a poor connection (OBD-Codes.com lists resistance; RepairPal lists wiring issues)
  4. Vacuum leaks, including the sensor's vacuum hose if it has one (OBD-Codes.com)
  5. Low fuel or running out of fuel (OBD-Codes.com)
  6. PCM failure (RepairPal; OBD-Codes.com says this is the last thing to suspect after everything else tests good)
  7. RepairPal lists fuel pump replacement as a common repair for this code

Diagnostic steps you can do

⚠ Before you start

Work on a cool engine, parked on level ground with the parking brake set. Never work under a car supported only by a jack.

  1. Make sure the tank isn't nearly empty; OBD-Codes.com lists low or no fuel as a cause.
  2. Look up technical service bulletins (TSBs) for your year, make, model and engine, and check for other fuel pump or fuel pressure codes. OBD-Codes.com says to diagnose those first and set P0194 aside.
  3. Find the fuel rail pressure sensor and inspect its connector and wiring for chafing, bare wires, burn marks, melted plastic or corroded (green) terminals. Clean corroded terminals with electrical contact cleaner and add dielectric grease (OBD-Codes.com).
  4. If the sensor has a vacuum hose to the intake manifold, check it and its connections for leaks and replace as needed (OBD-Codes.com).
  5. Clear the code with a scan tool and see if it returns. If it doesn't, the connection was likely the problem (OBD-Codes.com).
  6. Because the fault is intermittent, wiggle the connectors at the sensor and PCM with the engine running and watch the scan tool's fuel rail pressure reading for dropouts.

Typical fixes

  • Repair a loose, corroded or high-resistance connection or damaged wiring
  • Fix a leaking vacuum hose at the sensor or intake manifold
  • Replace the fuel rail pressure sensor (on some engines it is part of the fuel rail, per OBD-Codes.com)
  • Repair the cause of truly high or low fuel pressure, such as the fuel pump (RepairPal lists pump replacement as a common repair)

Repair costs vary widely by vehicle, region, parts brand and shop, so we don't quote prices. Get a written estimate after diagnosis.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • OBD-Codes.com's sensor check: with the key off, connect a mechanical fuel pressure gauge, then turn the key on and compare the gauge to the scan tool's fuel pressure reading. They should be within about 5 psi of each other. Fuel is under pressure and flammable, so leave this to a shop if you are not set up for it.
  • If everything tests good, inspect and wiggle-test the connectors at the PCM. OBD-Codes.com says a failed PCM is the most likely remaining cause, and recommends a hard reset (battery disconnect) before a PCM replacement is justified.
  • On diesel common-rail engines, OBD-Codes.com warns rail pressure is at least about 2,000 psi at warm idle and can exceed 35,000 psi under load; a leak can cut skin and cause blood poisoning, so have a professional replace the sensor

FAQs

Is P0194 a sensor problem or a real fuel pressure problem?

It can be either. OBD-Codes.com lists actual high or low fuel pressure, low fuel and vacuum leaks alongside a damaged sensor, and RepairPal names fuel pump replacement as a common repair. Comparing a mechanical gauge to the scan tool reading (they should agree within about 5 psi, per OBD-Codes.com) tells you which.

Can I drive with P0194?

RepairPal rates it urgent, and OBD-Codes.com lists lack of power and a crank-no-start. Limit driving and get it checked soon; tow it if the engine won't start or loses power.

Why does P0194 come and go?

It's an intermittent/erratic code, so the signal is dropping out or jumping rather than staying high or low. Loose or corroded connectors at the sensor or PCM are a common reason, which is why OBD-Codes.com suggests wiggle-testing the connections.
