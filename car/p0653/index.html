P0653: Sensor Reference Voltage B Circuit High (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-25 · Based on 2 cited source(s)

⚡ Quick answer

P0653 means the powertrain computer (PCM) has found that its 5-volt reference voltage supply labeled "B", which feeds one or more sensors, is higher than it should be. It usually shows up alongside a more specific sensor code, and the usual causes are wiring or connectors shorted to voltage, a bad sensor, or a PCM fault or programming error.

🚨 Safety first

A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire: slow down and stop driving soon. A red brake or oil pressure warning needs immediate attention. Pull over safely and don't keep driving.

What P0653 means

The PCM sends a steady reference voltage, typically 5 volts, to many sensors, and each sensor returns a changing signal the PCM reads. These supplies are grouped into circuits labeled A, B and so on. RepairPal says the PCM sends a 5-volt reference signal to the applicable engine sensors and sets P0653 when it detects a problem with that signal. OBD-Codes.com describes P0653 as the PCM seeing an input voltage on reference circuit "B" that is higher than a programmed limit. It is a generic code for OBD-II vehicles, and the repair steps vary by make and model. The sources differ on which sensors are involved: RepairPal talks about engine sensors and lists 'engine may not start' as the symptom, while OBD-Codes.com says the sensor has always been related to the automatic transmission, transfer case or front differential in its author's experience, so its symptoms are mostly shifting and 4WD problems. Which sensors share reference circuit B depends on the vehicle, so check a service manual for yours. OBD-Codes.com adds that P0653 is generally a support code for a more specific sensor code, that those codes should be diagnosed first (keeping the high reference voltage in mind) because P0653 usually disappears once they're fixed, and that a P0653 stored by itself suggests a PCM programming error. P0651 is the open-circuit code for the same circuit, P0652 is the circuit B low code, and P0643 is the high code for reference circuit "A".

Severity: can I keep driving?

Drive with caution, fix soon

Get it diagnosed soon. RepairPal rates P0653 Moderate, meaning it may not threaten safety right away but should be checked within a reasonable time. OBD-Codes.com says how serious it is depends on which sensor circuit has the high voltage and what other codes are stored with it. RepairPal notes the engine may not start, and if the car shifts badly, won't engage a gear or 4WD, or the speedometer stops working, drive gently and get it checked before relying on it.

Common symptoms

  • Check engine light on; OBD-Codes.com says some vehicles need several failed drive cycles before it lights (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Engine may not start (RepairPal)
  • Transmission shifting problems, or no shifting between sport and economy modes (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Delayed or no transmission engagement (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Won't switch between all-wheel and two-wheel drive, or transfer case won't shift from low to high (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Front differential or front hubs won't engage (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Erratic or dead speedometer or odometer (OBD-Codes.com)

Likely causes

Roughly most to least common, based on the sources below. Your vehicle may differ.

  1. Circuits or connectors shorted to voltage (OBD-Codes.com); wiring issue (RepairPal)
  2. Bad sensor on the reference circuit (OBD-Codes.com); engine sensor failure (RepairPal)
  3. Faulty engine computer (PCM) (RepairPal, OBD-Codes.com), or a PCM programming error when P0653 is stored alone (OBD-Codes.com)

Diagnostic steps you can do

⚠ Before you start

Work on a cool engine, parked on level ground with the parking brake set. Never work under a car supported only by a jack.

  1. Read all stored codes and write them down in the order they were stored, with any freeze frame data. Fix any more specific sensor codes first, as OBD-Codes.com recommends, since P0653 usually clears once those are fixed.
  2. Look over the sensor wiring and connectors for damage, burned spots, chafing or loose pins, and repair what you find. Then clear the code and test drive to see if it comes right back.
  3. If it returns, use a multimeter at the sensor connector. OBD-Codes.com says you should normally find about 5 volts and a good ground there.
  4. If the voltage and ground are present, test the sensor's resistance against the specs for your vehicle. If the reference voltage is high at the sensor, check resistance and continuity of the wiring between the sensor and the PCM for a short. Disconnect related control modules before any resistance checks, since OBD-Codes.com warns skipping that can damage the PCM.
  5. After a repair, clear the code and drive normally. OBD-Codes.com says if the PCM reaches readiness mode without the code returning, the repair worked.

Typical fixes

  • Repair wiring or connectors shorted to voltage in the reference circuit (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  • Replace a sensor that fails the manufacturer's resistance or continuity specs (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  • Reprogram or replace the PCM only after the wiring and sensors check out (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)

Repair costs vary widely by vehicle, region, parts brand and shop, so we don't quote prices. Get a written estimate after diagnosis.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • The engine won't start
  • The transmission won't engage or the 4WD system won't switch
  • The reference voltage at the sensor is high and you can't find the short
  • P0653 is the only code stored, which may point to a PCM programming issue

FAQs

Can I drive with P0653?

Often for a short time, but get it checked soon. RepairPal rates it Moderate, and OBD-Codes.com says the severity depends on which sensor circuit has high voltage. If the engine won't start, the transmission won't engage or 4WD won't work, don't rely on the car until it's fixed.

What's the difference between P0653 and P0652?

Both are about the same sensor reference voltage circuit "B". P0652 means the PCM sees the voltage on that circuit is too low, while P0653 means it's too high, which often points to a wire shorted to voltage.

Why is P0653 stored with other codes?

OBD-Codes.com says this code is generally a support code for a more specific sensor code, so fix those first; P0653 usually goes away once they're fixed. If P0653 is stored by itself, it suggests a PCM programming error.

How much does it cost to fix P0653?

Neither source gives a repair cost for P0653. A wiring or connector repair is much smaller than a sensor or PCM replacement, so find the cause before buying parts.
