P0193: Fuel Rail Pressure Sensor Circuit High (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-22 · Based on 2 cited source(s)

⚡ Quick answer

P0193 means the engine computer is getting a fuel rail pressure sensor signal that reads too high. Most often it is the sensor itself or its wiring and connector (for example the signal wire shorted to power or an open circuit), though RepairPal notes a bad fuel pressure regulator can also cause it. A scan tool reading compared with a mechanical fuel pressure gauge tells you whether the pressure is really high or the sensor is lying.

🚨 Safety first

A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire: slow down and stop driving soon. A red brake or oil pressure warning needs immediate attention. Pull over safely and don't keep driving.

What P0193 means

The fuel rail pressure (FRP) sensor tells the engine computer (PCM) how much pressure is in the fuel rail feeding the injectors, so it can meter the right amount of fuel. P0193 sets when that sensor's signal stays above a calibrated limit for a calibrated amount of time. OBD-Codes.com says it is a generic code for most fuel-injected engines, gas and diesel, since about 2000, and can be a mechanical or electrical fault depending on the vehicle. The two sources frame it a little differently: OBD-Codes.com treats it strictly as a circuit-high fault (signal shorted to power, open signal circuit, or a damaged sensor), while RepairPal says the high signal indicates an abnormally high fuel pressure condition and also describes the trigger as an erratic sensor signal, adding the fuel pressure regulator as a cause. A real pressure test settles which applies to your car.

Severity: can I keep driving?

Stop and fix promptly

RepairPal puts P0193 in its urgent category, meaning action is needed right away because it can cause severe damage or put the people in the car at risk. RepairPal lists hesitation and stalling (including dying at stop signs) as symptoms, and OBD-Codes.com says the engine may lack power or crank but not start. A stall in traffic is dangerous, so get it diagnosed promptly, and have it towed if the engine stalls or hesitates badly. Diesel common-rail systems run extremely high fuel pressure, so do not open or loosen anything on the fuel rail yourself.

Common symptoms

  • Check engine light on (both sources)
  • Lack of power (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Engine cranks but won't start (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Engine hesitation or stalling, including dying at stop signs (RepairPal)
  • Higher fuel consumption and an unusual exhaust smell (RepairPal)
  • A rich fuel trim code such as P0172 may also set (RepairPal)
  • Failed emissions test, or in rare cases no noticeable symptoms (RepairPal)

Likely causes

Roughly most to least common, based on the sources below. Your vehicle may differ.

  1. Faulty fuel rail pressure sensor (both sources)
  2. Sensor signal wire shorted to power, or an open signal circuit (OBD-Codes.com)
  3. Damaged wiring or a poor connection at the sensor connector (both sources)
  4. Faulty fuel pressure regulator (RepairPal)
  5. Less often: PCM failure, which OBD-Codes.com says can't be ruled out until the sensor has been replaced

Diagnostic steps you can do

⚠ Before you start

Work on a cool engine, parked on level ground with the parking brake set. Never work under a car supported only by a jack.

  1. Look up technical service bulletins (TSBs) for your year, make, model and engine (OBD-Codes.com).
  2. Find the fuel rail pressure sensor and inspect its connector and wiring for chafing, bare wires, burn marks, melted plastic or corroded (green) terminals. Clean corroded terminals with electrical contact cleaner and add dielectric grease (OBD-Codes.com).
  3. If the sensor has a vacuum hose to the intake manifold, check it for leaks. OBD-Codes.com says fuel coming out of that hose means the sensor is defective.
  4. Clear the code with a scan tool and see if it returns. If it doesn't, the connection was likely the problem (OBD-Codes.com).
  5. If your scan tool shows live data, watch the fuel rail pressure reading on a drive. RepairPal recommends confirming any high reading with a mechanical fuel pressure gauge before buying a sensor. Fuel is under pressure and flammable, so skip the gauge test if you are not set up for it.

Typical fixes

  • Repair a shorted or open signal wire, or a corroded or loose connector
  • Replace the fuel rail pressure sensor (on some engines it is part of the fuel rail, per OBD-Codes.com)
  • Replace a faulty fuel pressure regulator if a gauge confirms truly high pressure (RepairPal)

Repair costs vary widely by vehicle, region, parts brand and shop, so we don't quote prices. Get a written estimate after diagnosis.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • Testing the sensor circuit with a fused jumper and scan tool, or with a lab scope, needs a wiring diagram for your vehicle (both sources)
  • On diesel common-rail engines, OBD-Codes.com warns rail pressure is at least about 2,000 psi at warm idle and can exceed 35,000 psi under load; a leak can cut skin and cause blood poisoning, so have a professional replace the sensor
  • RepairPal lists common misdiagnoses: replacing the sensor when the real cause is the pressure regulator, the fuel pump, or a poor connection or chafed wiring

FAQs

Does P0193 mean my fuel pressure is actually too high?

Not necessarily. The code only says the sensor signal reads high. OBD-Codes.com lists electrical causes (a signal wire shorted to power, an open circuit, a bad sensor), while RepairPal says it can also reflect truly high pressure from a bad regulator. Compare the scan-tool reading with a mechanical gauge to find out.

Can I drive with P0193?

RepairPal rates it urgent, and both sources list stalling, hesitation, lack of power or a no-start. Limit driving and get it checked soon; tow it if the engine stalls.

Is P0193 always a bad sensor?

No. RepairPal warns that sensors are often replaced when the real problem is the fuel pressure regulator, the fuel pump, or a bad connection or chafed wire. Check the wiring and connector first.
