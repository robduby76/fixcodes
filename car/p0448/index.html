P0448: EVAP System Vent Valve/Solenoid Circuit Shorted (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-25 · Based on 2 cited source(s)

⚡ Quick answer

P0448 means the engine computer found a short in the circuit that controls the EVAP vent valve (vent solenoid), or, per OBD-Codes.com, a vent valve stuck closed. The vent valve lets fresh air into the charcoal canister and closes when the system tests itself for leaks. Both sources list a faulty vent valve, a wiring problem, or less often a computer fault as the causes.

🚨 Safety first

A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire: slow down and stop driving soon. A red brake or oil pressure warning needs immediate attention. Pull over safely and don't keep driving.

What P0448 means

The evaporative emission (EVAP) system catches fuel vapor from the fuel tank, stores it in a charcoal canister, and later draws it into the engine to be burned. The vent valve lets fresh air into the system so it isn't under constant vacuum, and the engine computer closes it to seal the system during leak tests. OBD-Codes.com describes P0448 as a generic OBD-II code (1996 and newer) that sets when the computer detects that the circuit controlling the vent valve or solenoid is shorted, and says the code will also set when the valve is stuck closed. RepairPal defines it as Evaporative Emission System Vent Control Circuit Shorted, but its explanation says the computer sets it when it detects the EVAP system venting when it should not, which is closer to a valve stuck open. The sources disagree on that point, so check your vehicle's service information for the exact rule. P0446 is the general vent control circuit code, P0447 is the vent control circuit open code, and P0449 is the vent valve/solenoid circuit malfunction code.

Severity: can I keep driving?

Drive with caution, fix soon

Get it diagnosed within a reasonable time. RepairPal rates P0448 Moderate, meaning it may not threaten safety or drivability right away but should still be checked. OBD-Codes.com says you will likely notice nothing besides the check engine light. It can keep the car from passing an emissions inspection. If you smell raw fuel, get it checked sooner, and never ignore a strong fuel smell.

Common symptoms

  • Check engine (service engine soon) light on (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Usually no other symptoms; OBD-Codes.com says you will likely not notice anything else

Likely causes

Roughly most to least common, based on the sources below. Your vehicle may differ.

  1. Faulty EVAP vent valve or solenoid (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  2. Wiring or circuit problems, such as a rubbed-through or chafed harness with wires shorted to each other, to ground or to power (OBD-Codes.com; RepairPal lists a wiring issue)
  3. Vent valve pintle sticking from fuel residue (OBD-Codes.com)
  4. PCM/ECM fault, less common (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)

Diagnostic steps you can do

⚠ Before you start

Work on a cool engine, parked on level ground with the parking brake set. Never work under a car supported only by a jack.

  1. Read all stored codes. If other EVAP codes are present (such as P0446, P0447 or P0449), note them, since they can point to the same valve or circuit.
  2. Check the fuse that powers the vent solenoid, if your vehicle has one (OBD-Codes.com).
  3. Find the vent valve, usually at or near the charcoal canister close to the fuel tank, and inspect it for cracks or damage (OBD-Codes.com).
  4. Inspect the vent valve's wiring harness for rubbed-through or chafed spots, and check that the two wires aren't shorted to each other, to ground or to power (OBD-Codes.com).
  5. With a multimeter, check the valve's coil resistance against the manufacturer's specification. OBD-Codes.com says it is usually low, and a value too high or too low means the coil inside the valve is bad.

Typical fixes

  • Replace a faulty EVAP vent valve or solenoid (OBD-Codes.com; RepairPal lists vent valve failure first). OBD-Codes.com says this was the fix in many Dodge, Chrysler and Jeep cases
  • Repair shorted or chafed wiring in the vent valve circuit (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Replace a blown fuse, after finding out why it blew (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Remove and clean a vent valve that is sticking from fuel residue, then retest (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Install an updated valve assembly if the manufacturer has released one; OBD-Codes.com says GM released an updated valve for some pickups

Repair costs vary widely by vehicle, region, parts brand and shop, so we don't quote prices. Get a written estimate after diagnosis.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • Testing continuity from the valve back to the engine computer needs a multimeter and the vehicle's wiring diagram (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Commanding the valve open and closed while checking power and ground at the connector needs an advanced scan tool (OBD-Codes.com)
  • The code comes back after replacing the vent valve, which points to wiring or the engine computer
  • You smell raw fuel, or the fuel tank or filler neck looks damaged

FAQs

Is P0448 serious?

RepairPal rates it Moderate, and OBD-Codes.com says you will likely notice nothing besides the check engine light. It usually isn't an emergency, but it can fail an emissions inspection, so get it diagnosed within a reasonable time.

Is P0448 always a bad vent valve?

No. A faulty vent valve is the first cause both sources list, and OBD-Codes.com says it was the fix in many Dodge, Chrysler and Jeep cases, but shorted wiring, a sticking valve or, less often, the engine computer can also set it.

What's the difference between P0448 and P0447?

Both involve the EVAP vent control circuit. P0448 is the shorted-circuit code, while P0447 is the open-circuit code. P0446 and P0449 are the related general vent circuit codes.

How much does it cost to fix P0448?

Neither source gives a repair cost. It can range from a fuse or wiring repair to a new vent valve, so get a diagnosis and a written estimate first.
