P0171: System Too Lean (Bank 1) (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-26 · Based on 2 cited source(s)

⚡ Quick answer

P0171 means the oxygen sensor on Bank 1 sees too much oxygen in the exhaust, and the engine computer can't add enough fuel to hold the ideal 14.7:1 air-fuel ratio. A dirty MAF sensor or a vacuum leak is often the cause.

🚨 Safety first

A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire: slow down and stop driving soon. A red brake or oil pressure warning needs immediate attention. Pull over safely and don't keep driving.

🚗 Can I keep driving?

Usually yes, for a short time. OBD-Codes.com says you'll more than likely not notice drivability problems, but RepairPal rates P0171 Urgent and lists lack of power, coughing or misfiring, and rough idle as possible symptoms. RepairPal also says a lean engine can misfire, which can damage the catalytic converter, so don't leave it for long. Stop driving soon if the check engine light flashes or the engine runs very roughly.

What to check first

In order, cheapest and easiest first. Stop when the code stays away.

  1. Read all stored codes and the freeze-frame data. If P0174 (Bank 2) is stored too, both banks are lean, which points to something they share such as the MAF sensor, a vacuum leak or fuel delivery. OBD-Codes.com says the two codes often appear together.
  2. Look over the air intake path. RepairPal says to keep the air filter and its housing clean and to look for a torn intake air boot.
  3. Check the vacuum and PCV hoses for cracks or loose fittings, and check the PCV valve and the dipstick seal. RepairPal says a torn PCV hose, a torn intake boot or a dipstick that doesn't seal can let in unmetered air; OBD-Codes.com also lists a faulty or stuck-open PCV valve.
  4. Look for an exhaust leak between the engine and the first oxygen sensor, which OBD-Codes.com lists as a possible cause.
  5. Clean the MAF sensor with a cleaner that's safe for it, being careful not to damage it, and let it dry before reinstalling. OBD-Codes.com says cleaning it and fixing vacuum leaks often fixes the code, and RepairPal says dirt, dust or oil on the sensing wire can set P0171.
  6. Ask a dealer or check for a technical service bulletin or control module software update for your vehicle. RepairPal says to always check, because on worn engines the stock fuel map can drift lean.
  7. If you have a scan tool, watch long-term fuel trim at idle, at 3,000 rpm unloaded and at 3,000 rpm with at least 50 percent load, and see which range failed in freeze frame (RepairPal).
  8. Check fuel delivery: a plugged fuel filter or weak fuel pump can cause it, so check fuel filter condition and fuel pressure with a gauge. OBD-Codes.com also suggests a smoke test to find leaks, and RepairPal lists oxygen sensors as the common misdiagnosis, so don't start there.

Quick FAQ

Can a dirty MAF sensor or air filter cause P0171?

Yes. OBD-Codes.com lists a dirty or faulty MAF sensor first among the causes and says that cleaning it and fixing vacuum leaks often fixes the code. RepairPal says dirt, dust or oil on the sensing wire can set P0171 and that the air filter and its housing should be kept clean so a new sensor doesn't fail the same way. RepairPal adds that cleaning may only hold off the problem, and a failing MAF sensor eventually needs replacing.

Where do vacuum leaks usually hide?

RepairPal lists a torn PCV hose, a torn intake air boot, a dipstick that doesn't seal (it's part of the PCV system), a sticking or leaking EGR valve, and a leaking EGR or intake manifold gasket. On a V6 or V8, if only one bank is lean, RepairPal says a defective intake manifold gasket or a cracked manifold is possible. OBD-Codes.com suggests a smoke test if you have access to one.

What do the fuel trim numbers mean for P0171?

Fuel trim is how much extra fuel the computer is adding to compensate. RepairPal says long-term trim should ideally be within about 1 to 2 percent, and that P0171 sets when it is compensating anywhere from 15 percent to as much as 35 percent. It suggests reading long-term trim at idle, at 3,000 rpm unloaded and at 3,000 rpm with at least 50 percent load, then checking freeze frame to see which range failed.

What P0171 means

The upstream (front) oxygen sensor on Bank 1 reads the air-fuel mixture leaving the cylinders. The PCM adjusts fuel to keep the ratio near 14.7:1. When it can't, because there's too much air, it sets P0171. On V6/V8/V10 engines, Bank 1 is the side with cylinder #1. P0171 is very similar to P0174 (Bank 2), and the two often show up together.

Severity: can I keep driving?

Drive with caution, fix soon

Usually drivable short-term; you may not notice anything. But a lean engine can hesitate, knock or run rough, and lean conditions are a common cause of misfires. Get it fixed soon rather than ignoring it.

Common symptoms

  • Check engine light on (often the only symptom)
  • Lack of power
  • Spark knock (detonation)
  • Rough idle
  • Hesitation or surge on acceleration
  • 'Coughing' or misfiring, and trouble idling when warm or at a stoplight (RepairPal)

Likely causes

Roughly most to least common, based on the sources below. Your vehicle may differ.

  1. Dirty or faulty mass air flow (MAF) sensor (over-oiled air filters can contaminate it)
  2. Vacuum leak after the MAF sensor
  3. Cracked vacuum or PCV hose or connection
  4. Faulty or stuck-open PCV valve
  5. Control module software that needs updating (RepairPal). Check for TSBs or a dealer reflash.
  6. Failed upstream oxygen sensor (Bank 1 Sensor 1)
  7. Sticking, plugged, dirty or failed fuel injector
  8. Low fuel pressure: a plugged fuel filter or weak fuel pump
  9. Exhaust leak between the engine and the first O2 sensor

Diagnostic steps you can do

⚠ Before you start

Work on a cool engine, parked on level ground with the parking brake set. Never work under a car supported only by a jack.

  1. Clean the MAF sensor with a cleaner that's safe for it. Be careful not to damage it, and let it dry before reinstalling.
  2. Inspect all vacuum and PCV hoses, and every hose and connection in the intake, for cracks or loose fittings.
  3. Check the intake manifold gaskets for leaks.
  4. If you have a scanner with live data, watch short- and long-term fuel trims.
  5. Check for a dirty fuel filter and, with a gauge, the fuel pressure.
  6. Ask a dealer, or search NHTSA, for technical service bulletins. RepairPal lists outdated control-module software as a common cause.

Typical fixes

  • Clean or replace the MAF sensor
  • Repair vacuum, PCV or intake leaks
  • Replace a faulty PCV valve or O2 sensor
  • Restore fuel pressure (filter, pump or regulator)
  • Repair or replace faulty injectors

Repair costs vary widely by vehicle, region, parts brand and shop, so we don't quote prices. Get a written estimate after diagnosis.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • You can't find the leak: a shop can run a smoke test
  • Fuel pressure testing needs a gauge and the right adapter
  • The code returns after the cheap fixes

FAQs

What fixes P0171 most often?

OBD-Codes.com says cleaning the MAF sensor and fixing vacuum leaks often fixes it, though not always.

Why do I have P0171 and P0174 together?

Both banks are lean, which points to something they share, such as a MAF problem, a vacuum leak, or low fuel pressure.

Is P0171 urgent?

Sources differ. RepairPal rates it 'Urgent'; OBD-Codes.com says you more than likely won't notice drivability problems. Both agree the oxygen sensor is a common misdiagnosis, so diagnose before replacing it. Note: lean means too much air (oxygen), not enough fuel.

Should I replace the O2 sensor?

RepairPal lists oxygen sensors as the common misdiagnosis for P0171. Check for vacuum leaks, the MAF and fuel delivery first.
