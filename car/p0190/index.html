P0190: Fuel Rail Pressure Sensor A Circuit (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-25 · Based on 2 cited source(s)

⚡ Quick answer

P0190 means the engine computer sees a problem with the signal from the fuel rail pressure sensor A circuit. Common causes are a damaged sensor, an open or high-resistance 5-volt reference wire, other wiring faults, or less often the engine computer (PCM).

🚨 Safety first

A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire: slow down and stop driving soon. A red brake or oil pressure warning needs immediate attention. Pull over safely and don't keep driving.

What P0190 means

The powertrain control module (PCM) uses the fuel rail pressure (FRP) sensor to know how much pressure is reaching the fuel injectors, so it can meter fuel for power and economy (RepairPal). The two sources describe the trigger a little differently: OBD-Codes.com says the code is concerned with the sensor's signal falling below a calibrated limit for a calibrated amount of time, while RepairPal says the PCM sets it when it detects an erratic signal from the sensor. OBD-Codes.com adds that it can be a mechanical or an electrical fault depending on the manufacturer, fuel type and fuel system. It is a generic OBD-II code that applies to most fuel-injected gas and diesel engines (OBD-Codes.com names Volvo, Ford, GMC and VW among others), and the exact test steps and wire colors vary by vehicle. Related codes P0191 (range/performance), P0192 (circuit low), P0193 (circuit high) and P0194 (intermittent/erratic) narrow the same circuit down further.

Severity: can I keep driving?

Stop and fix promptly

Get it diagnosed soon. RepairPal rates P0190 Urgent, meaning it can cause severe damage or be unsafe, and OBD-Codes.com lists lack of power and an engine that cranks but won't start among the symptoms. If the engine stalls or loses power, keep driving to a minimum. On common-rail diesels, OBD-Codes.com warns that rail pressure can run from about 2,000 psi at idle to well over 35,000 psi under load, enough to cut skin, so leave fuel-system work on those engines to a professional.

Common symptoms

  • Check engine light on (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Lack of power (OBD-Codes.com), or decreased engine performance (RepairPal)
  • Engine cranks but won't start (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Increased fuel consumption (RepairPal)

Likely causes

Roughly most to least common, based on the sources below. Your vehicle may differ.

  1. Damaged or failed fuel rail pressure sensor (both sources)
  2. Open circuit or excessive resistance in the sensor's 5-volt reference (VREF) wire (OBD-Codes.com)
  3. Other wiring or connector problems, such as chafed, corroded or burnt terminals (OBD-Codes.com inspection steps; RepairPal lists a wiring issue)
  4. Failed PCM (RepairPal lists it as a cause; OBD-Codes.com says a bad PCM is the likely answer only after all wiring and sensor tests pass)
  5. The sources differ on the repair: RepairPal names fuel pump replacement as the common repair, even though its cause list is the PCM, the sensor and wiring, while OBD-Codes.com focuses on the sensor, its wiring and the PCM and doesn't mention the pump

Diagnostic steps you can do

⚠ Before you start

Work on a cool engine, parked on level ground with the parking brake set. Never work under a car supported only by a jack.

  1. Check for technical service bulletins (TSBs) for your vehicle first; OBD-Codes.com says a known fix may exist.
  2. Find the fuel rail pressure sensor and inspect its wiring and connector for chafing, bare wires, burn spots, melted plastic, or corroded or green terminals. Clean terminals with electrical contact cleaner and apply dielectric grease (OBD-Codes.com).
  3. Clear the code and see if it returns. If it doesn't, the connection was most likely the problem (OBD-Codes.com).
  4. With the sensor unplugged, use a digital multimeter to confirm about 5 volts on the supply wire and on the signal wire. Seeing 12 volts where 5 should be points to a wiring short or PCM problem (OBD-Codes.com).
  5. Check the sensor ground with a test light connected to battery positive, and wiggle the harness to look for an intermittent connection (OBD-Codes.com).

Typical fixes

  • Repair open, shorted or high-resistance wiring, or clean and reseal corroded connector terminals (OBD-Codes.com; RepairPal lists wiring)
  • Replace the fuel rail pressure sensor (both sources list it; on some diesels it is part of the fuel rail, per OBD-Codes.com)
  • Fuel pump replacement is the common repair RepairPal lists, so have fuel pressure checked when the sensor and wiring test good
  • Replace or reprogram the PCM only after everything else tests good; OBD-Codes.com recommends a battery-disconnect reset before justifying a PCM

Repair costs vary widely by vehicle, region, parts brand and shop, so we don't quote prices. Get a written estimate after diagnosis.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • The vehicle is a common-rail diesel, where OBD-Codes.com recommends a professional install the sensor because of extreme fuel pressure
  • The engine stalls, won't start, or loses power while driving
  • The sensor is built into the fuel rail on your engine
  • Wiring and sensor tests pass and the code returns, which can point to the PCM or a fuel delivery problem

FAQs

Is P0190 serious?

Treat it as serious. RepairPal rates it Urgent, and OBD-Codes.com lists lack of power and a crank-no-start condition as possible symptoms.

Is P0190 always a bad fuel pressure sensor?

No. Both sources list the sensor, but OBD-Codes.com points to the 5-volt reference wire and connectors too, and RepairPal also lists wiring and the PCM. Inspect and test the wiring before buying parts.

How much does it cost to fix P0190?

Neither source gives a repair cost. It depends on whether the fix is a connector, wiring, the sensor, the fuel pump or the PCM, so get a diagnosis or a written estimate first.
