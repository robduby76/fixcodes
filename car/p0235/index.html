P0235: Turbocharger/Supercharger Boost Sensor A Circuit (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-26 · Based on 2 cited source(s)

⚡ Quick answer

P0235 means the engine computer isn't happy with the signal from the boost pressure sensor: the boost it reads doesn't match what the computer expects. The two sources disagree on where to start. OBD-Codes.com checks the turbo, wastegate and boost leaks first, while RepairPal lists wiring, the turbo, an exhaust leak and a brake booster pressure sensor.

🚨 Safety first

A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire: slow down and stop driving soon. A red brake or oil pressure warning needs immediate attention. Pull over safely and don't keep driving.

What P0235 means

On a turbocharged or supercharged engine, a boost pressure sensor tells the engine control module (ECM or PCM) how much boost the turbo or supercharger is actually making. RepairPal says P0235 sets when the PCM detects an incorrect signal from that sensor. OBD-Codes.com describes it as an unacceptable difference between the boost the ECM has programmed for a given RPM and the value the sensor reports; the two must match within close limits. P0235 is the general circuit code for boost sensor A. P0236 is its range/performance code, P0237 is circuit low and P0238 is circuit high. OBD-Codes.com notes P0239 is the same code for boost sensor B. The sources' cause lists differ: OBD-Codes.com focuses mostly on mechanical turbo problems (wastegate, bearings, oil supply, boost leaks, intercooler) before the sensor wiring, while RepairPal lists a brake booster pressure sensor failure and an exhaust leak, which OBD-Codes.com does not mention.

Severity: can I keep driving?

Stop and fix promptly

Get it checked soon and drive gently until you do. RepairPal rates P0235 Urgent, meaning immediate action is needed. OBD-Codes.com says turbo problems should be addressed as soon as possible because a turbocharged engine greatly exaggerates engine problems.

Common symptoms

  • Check engine light on (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Decreased engine performance or lack of acceleration (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  • Lack of boost from the turbo or supercharger (RepairPal); OBD-Codes.com says a boost gauge may read too low or too high
  • Increased fuel consumption (RepairPal)
  • Whining or rattling from the turbo or its piping, or hissing at the wastegate (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Smoke from the exhaust or fouled spark plugs (OBD-Codes.com)
  • A knock sensor code or unusually high engine temperature at cruise (OBD-Codes.com)

Likely causes

Roughly most to least common, based on the sources below. Your vehicle may differ.

  1. A wiring problem: a short or open in the harness between the boost sensor and the computer, or a poor connection at the sensor (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  2. A faulty boost sensor, or a faulty 5 volt reference driver in the ECM (OBD-Codes.com)
  3. A turbocharger or supercharger failure, such as worn bearings, damaged blades, excess shaft end play, a cracked housing or an oil seal leak (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  4. A wastegate stuck open, stuck closed or leaking (OBD-Codes.com)
  5. Poor oil supply to the turbo bearing from a blocked feed or return line (OBD-Codes.com)
  6. A boost or vacuum leak: loose intake pipe connections, a vacuum leak at the intake manifold or a defective intercooler (OBD-Codes.com)
  7. A dirty air filter (OBD-Codes.com)
  8. An exhaust leak, including loose exhaust manifold to turbo bolts (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  9. A brake booster pressure sensor failure (RepairPal only)
  10. A faulty PCM (RepairPal; OBD-Codes.com mentions the ECM 5 volt reference)

Diagnostic steps you can do

⚠ Before you start

Work on a cool engine, parked on level ground with the parking brake set. Never work under a car supported only by a jack.

  1. Scan for other codes first and make sure the engine runs well with no misfires. OBD-Codes.com says to confirm there are no misfire or knock sensor codes before blaming the turbo.
  2. On a cold engine, check the hose clamps at the turbo outlet, intercooler and throttle body for tightness, and look for intake and vacuum hose leaks.
  3. Check that the turbo is tight on its exhaust flange and look for exhaust leaks around the turbo.
  4. Check the air filter and replace it if it's dirty.
  5. Inspect the oil feed and return lines to the turbo for leaks or kinks. With the engine cool and the outlet hose off, OBD-Codes.com says to look for blades rubbing the housing, bent blades or oil inside the turbo, and spin it by hand to feel for grinding.
  6. If the turbo checks out, test the boost sensor circuit with a multimeter using your service manual. OBD-Codes.com says to confirm the 5 volt reference from the computer (no voltage points to the harness or the computer) and then check that the sensor's signal voltage rises with RPM (no rise points to the sensor).
  7. OBD-Codes.com also describes vacuum and boost gauge tests with specific numbers, but boost specs vary a lot between engines, so use the figures in your vehicle's service manual.

Typical fixes

  • Repair shorted or open wiring or a bad connector at the boost sensor (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  • Tighten or replace loose or leaking intake and intercooler connections, and fix vacuum leaks (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Repair an exhaust leak (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  • Replace a dirty air filter (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Replace the boost sensor if the reference voltage is good but the signal doesn't change with RPM (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Repair a sticking or leaking wastegate (OBD-Codes.com)
  • Repair or replace the turbocharger only after the checks above (OBD-Codes.com, RepairPal)
  • Replace or reprogram the PCM only as a last step (RepairPal)

Repair costs vary widely by vehicle, region, parts brand and shop, so we don't quote prices. Get a written estimate after diagnosis.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • The engine has very low power or goes into limp mode
  • You hear new turbo whine, grinding or rattling, or see blue or heavy smoke
  • The turbo shows oil inside, blade damage or shaft play
  • You don't have a service manual, multimeter or boost gauge
  • The code comes back after the wiring and sensor check out

FAQs

Can I drive with a P0235 code?

Only gently and not for long. RepairPal rates it Urgent, and OBD-Codes.com says turbo problems should be addressed as soon as possible because a turbocharged engine exaggerates engine problems.

Is P0235 a bad boost sensor or a bad turbo?

It can be either, or neither. OBD-Codes.com starts with turbo, wastegate and boost-leak checks and then tests the sensor circuit, while RepairPal lists wiring, the turbo, an exhaust leak, a brake booster pressure sensor and the PCM. Check the cheap things first before buying parts.

What is the difference between P0235, P0236, P0237 and P0238?

All four are boost sensor A codes. P0235 is the general circuit code, P0236 is range/performance, P0237 is circuit low and P0238 is circuit high. P0239 is the same as P0235 for boost sensor B.

Should I turn up the boost to fix P0235?

No. OBD-Codes.com warns never to tighten the wastegate or modify a stock turbo engine to raise boost, because the fuel and timing won't adapt and engine damage can happen.

How much does it cost to fix P0235?

Neither source gives a repair cost. A loose clamp, wiring repair or sensor is usually far cheaper than a turbo, so get a diagnosis or written estimate before buying parts.
