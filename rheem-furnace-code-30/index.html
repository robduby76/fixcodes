Rheem Furnace Code 30: Open Fuse on the Control Board (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-23 · Based on 2 manufacturer & service sources

⚡ Quick answer

Code 30 on a Rheem or Ruud gas furnace means the small fuse on the furnace control board has blown. That fuse protects the 24-volt thermostat wiring, and it almost always blows because something shorted that low-voltage wiring to ground or to the common wire. The furnace won't run in any mode, so no heat, cooling or fan, until the short is found and the fuse is replaced. Replacing the fuse without finding the short usually just blows the new one.

What the code means

Rheem's installation manuals for its variable-speed furnace controls (for example the 90%+ R96V and the 80% 802V) list fault code 30 as "Open Fuse". The integrated furnace control has a fuse that protects the low-voltage (24 VAC) circuits from shorts between 24VAC and ground or common, and the control displays code 30 when that fuse has opened. Rheem calls it a critical fault: the furnace will not operate in any mode, the code is displayed and no other operation can take place. The cause Rheem gives is an electrical short from the 24 VAC side to ground or common, and the solution is to repair the short and then replace the fuse. Because the thermostat, the outdoor air conditioner or heat pump connection and accessories all run on that 24-volt circuit, the short can be anywhere along those wires, not just inside the furnace.

Likely causes

  • A short between the 24 VAC wire and ground or the common wire somewhere in the low-voltage circuit, which is the cause Rheem's fault table gives (Rheem R96V and 802V manuals)
  • Thermostat wiring that's pinched, chewed or has bare strands touching at the thermostat or at the furnace terminal block, often right after a new thermostat was installed
  • Damaged low-voltage wire running to the outdoor air conditioner or heat pump, for example cut by a mower or trimmer, chewed by animals or rubbed through against the cabinet
  • A shorted 24-volt accessory or component on the circuit, such as a contactor coil in the outdoor unit, a humidifier, a zone board or a condensate pump safety switch wired in
  • Wires accidentally touched to the metal cabinet or to each other while the furnace was being worked on with the power on

Safe DIY checks

⚠ Before you start

Turn off power at the furnace/HVAC service switch and the breaker before opening panels. Shut the gas valve before any work near the burners, and never disconnect or alter the gas line. If you smell gas, leave the house and call your gas utility.

  1. Think about what changed. A new thermostat, outdoor work near the AC line, or recent furnace service points you straight at the most likely spot for the short.
  2. Turn off power to the furnace at its switch or breaker before touching anything. Shorting wires with the power on is one of the ways this fuse blows.
  3. At the thermostat, pull it off its base and check that no bare copper strands are touching each other or the wall plate, and that the wires are firmly in their own terminals.
  4. At the furnace, remove the access door and look at the thermostat wires where they connect to the control board terminal block and where the outdoor unit's wires join them. Look for bare wire, loose strands, nicked insulation or wires pressed against sheet metal.
  5. Walk the outdoor unit's thin control wire from the house to the condenser and look for obvious damage, such as a cut or chewed wire or one crushed under something.
  6. If you find and fix an obvious problem, a technician or an experienced DIYer can replace the fuse on the board with one of the exact same type and amp rating as the old one. Never use a higher-rated fuse or bypass it.
  7. If the new fuse blows again, or you can't find the short, stop and call an HVAC technician. Repeatedly replacing fuses can damage the transformer or control board.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • Finding the short in the 24 VAC circuit, as Rheem's fault table directs, by isolating the thermostat, outdoor unit and accessory wiring one branch at a time.
  • Repairing or replacing damaged thermostat or outdoor control wire, or a shorted contactor coil or accessory.
  • Replacing the board fuse with the correct type and rating only after the short is repaired, then confirming the furnace runs through a full heat and cool cycle.
  • Checking the transformer and control board only if the fuse keeps blowing with all field wiring disconnected. Rheem notes the model data card should stay with the furnace if the control is replaced.
