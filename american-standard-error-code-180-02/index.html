Code: 180.02❄️ Trane HVAC System

American Standard / Trane Error 180.02: Low Supply Voltage Fault (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-25 · Based on 2 manufacturer & service sources

⚡ Quick answer

Err 180.02 on an American Standard or Trane variable-speed outdoor unit means the compressor drive saw its incoming supply voltage drop below 175 volts AC and shut the compressor down for a 5-minute soft lockout. Trane lists a brownout or power flicker as the usual cause and rates it a Major alert. The unit itself is usually fine; the question is why the power sagged. Note when it happens, check the breaker and disconnect, and have a technician measure the voltage at the outdoor unit while it runs if it keeps coming back.

What the code means

American Standard and Trane are sister brands, and their communicating variable-speed outdoor units (Trane's alert guide names model families such as 4TWV, 4TTV, 4A6V and 4A7V) share the same alert code list. In that list the 180 family is the compressor drive watching its incoming line voltage. Trane's System Alert Guide (August 2020) describes ERR.180.02 LOW SUPPLY VOLTAGE FAULT as: supply voltage is less than 175 VAC, with a 5 minute soft lockout. Its listed possible causes are a brown out or power flicker, with the advice to look for other alerts in the same time period, since 178.01 (DC bus voltage low) will often accompany it. The ComfortLink II alert table rates CL2.180.02 (LOW VOLT CO) as a Major alert. For context, 180.00 and 180.01 are derate alerts: below about 200 VAC, or under high load, the drive reduces compressor speed instead of stopping. Trane's table gives 187 to 253 VAC as the supply range to verify when the related 178.01 low DC bus alert appears. Several other drive alerts (181.11, 181.14 and 181.15) list a power interrupt as a cause and note that 180.02 will often accompany them.

Likely causes

  • A brownout, power flicker or short outage from the utility. This is the cause Trane names first, and it often shows up in the alert history after a storm or on hot afternoons.
  • Voltage sag inside the house: a loose or corroded connection at the breaker, outdoor disconnect or unit terminals, or undersized wiring that drops voltage when the compressor ramps up. Trane's guide doesn't name house wiring directly, but anything that lowers voltage at the unit below 175 VAC will trigger the alert.
  • Heavy loads on the same service starting at the same moment (an electric range, dryer, water heater or well pump), which can pull the voltage down briefly. This is general electrical background, not a Trane-listed cause.
  • Rarely, a drive problem. When the alert stays current and the measured supply voltage is normal, Trane points technicians to its drive diagnostics.

Safe DIY checks

⚠ Before you start

Turn off power at the furnace/HVAC service switch and the breaker before opening panels.

  1. Look at when it happens and write it down: one alert after a storm or power blip is normal, and the unit restarts on its own after the 5-minute lockout.
  2. Check the thermostat's alert history for companion codes, especially 178.01 (DC bus voltage low) or 181.11, 181.14 and 181.15. Trane says these often appear together with 180.02 after a power interrupt.
  3. Make sure the outdoor unit's breaker is fully on and not warm or discolored, and that the outdoor disconnect is fully seated. Don't open the disconnect box or panel to tighten anything with power on.
  4. Notice whether lights dim when the outdoor unit starts or when big appliances turn on. Report that to an electrician or your utility; frequent dips point to a supply problem, not the heat pump or AC.
  5. If neighbors had flickering power at the same time, call the utility and mention it. Low voltage from the street is their fix, not an HVAC repair.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • The alert repeats with no outage or storm to explain it. A technician measures line voltage at the disconnect and unit terminals while the compressor is running, ideally logging it over time, and looks for a sag below 175 VAC.
  • Loose, burned or undersized wiring between the panel and the outdoor unit, which needs an electrician.
  • The alert stays current even though measured voltage is normal. Trane's guidance for the related drive alerts is to run drive diagnostics and consult the Variable Speed Outdoor Unit Technical Service Manual, and to call Trane tech support before replacing the drive.
  • A utility-side voltage problem that your power company needs to correct.
