Code: 89❄️ Trane HVAC System

Trane / American Standard Error 89: Equipment Missing (Meaning, Causes, Fixes)

Reviewed 2026-10-25 · Based on 3 manufacturer & service sources

⚡ Quick answer

ERR 89 on a Trane or American Standard communicating thermostat means Equipment Missing: a unit the thermostat found on the communication bus when the system was first powered up (the outdoor unit, the indoor unit, or a bank of electric heat) has stopped reporting. The most common causes are a tripped outdoor breaker or disconnect, a blown fuse or dead transformer at the outdoor unit, or broken communication wiring. Check that every breaker and disconnect is on, then power-cycle the system once.

What the code means

Trane's communicating systems (ComfortLink II and the matching American Standard AccuLink controls) auto-discover every communicating component the first time they are powered up and remember them: the outdoor unit, the indoor unit, and each bank of electric heat. Trane's System Alert Guide (August 2020) lists ERR.089.00 as Equipment Missing, meaning equipment that was auto-discovered at start-up is no longer reporting, and ERR.089.01 as Equipment Not Responding to Heat/Cool Demand, meaning expected equipment did not answer the thermostat's heat or cool demand message. For both, the guide's check is the DC voltage between the D and B terminals at every communicating component: about 12 VDC means good communication and about 16 VDC means communication is lost. Trane's ComfortLink II alert table lists both CL2.089.00 and CL2.089.01 as Critical, under the short name Config Fault. A Trane communicating thermostat service manual (published on ManualsLib under ACONT900AC43UA) adds the likely causes: the outdoor unit breaker tripped, the outdoor transformer or fuse is open, the field control wiring to the outdoor unit is open (16 VDC between D and B at the outdoor unit means it isn't communicating), or an auto-discovered auxiliary heat circuit is no longer sensed because a heater contactor coil, thermal limit or its connection to the control board is open. It also notes that ERR 89 may be followed by ERR 79, ERR 53 or ERR 126 depending on when the fault happened. If you replaced or removed equipment on purpose, the related alert is 126 (Equipment Change); see our Trane 126 page.

Likely causes

  • The outdoor unit's breaker tripped or its outdoor disconnect was pulled or switched off
  • An open fuse or a failed transformer at the outdoor unit, so its control board has no power
  • Broken, loose or corroded communication wiring (the D and B wires) between the thermostat, indoor unit and outdoor unit
  • An electric heat bank that the system discovered at start-up is no longer sensed, for example an open heater contactor coil, an open thermal limit, or a loose connection to the control board
  • A communicating component that is powered but not answering the thermostat's heat or cool demand (089.01)

Safe DIY checks

⚠ Before you start

Turn off power at the furnace/HVAC service switch and the breaker before opening panels.

  1. Note the full code if your thermostat shows it (89.00 or 89.01) and which equipment the thermostat's alert or diagnostic screen names, if any.
  2. Check the breakers for the outdoor unit, the indoor unit or furnace, and any electric heat. Reset anything that's tripped once. If a breaker trips again right away, leave it off and call a technician.
  3. Make sure the outdoor disconnect box next to the condenser or heat pump is fully seated or switched on, and the furnace or air handler service switch is on.
  4. Power-cycle the system once: turn off power to the indoor and outdoor units for about a minute, then turn it back on and give the thermostat a few minutes to find everything again.
  5. Look at the thin thermostat wire where it enters the outdoor unit and the indoor unit for anything chewed, cut, pinched or corroded. Don't open electrical panels unless you're comfortable doing so with the power off.
  6. If you're comfortable with a multimeter, Trane's check is the DC voltage between the D and B terminals at each communicating component. About 12 VDC is good; about 16 VDC at a unit means that unit isn't communicating.

🛠 When to call a pro

  • ERR 89 comes back after the breakers are on and the system has been power-cycled.
  • A breaker trips again, or the outdoor unit has no power at all with its breaker and disconnect on (possible open fuse or failed transformer).
  • You measure about 16 VDC between D and B at a component, or the communication wiring needs to be traced or replaced.
  • The system has electric heat and a heater contactor, thermal limit or board connection needs testing.
  • ERR 89 is followed by other codes such as 79, 53 or 126, which a technician should read together.
